**Status: draft.** Part of a series on a personal project that indexes spoken language
so you can look up how a word is actually used out loud. This post is about one
subproblem: given a video's audio and nothing else, find the stretches that are
spoken in the language you care about — reliably enough to show them to a learner.

Every number below comes from four real videos and thirty synthetic ones. Where the
result contradicted what I expected, the wrong expectation is still in the text,
because that is the interesting part.

---

## The problem

I am building a search index over spoken language. You type a word, you get back
clips of people actually saying it in a sentence — not a dictionary example, a real
one, with the audio.

The corpus is built from captions. That works until it doesn't: plenty of channels
have no captions in the target language, and language-teaching channels often caption
only the language the lesson is taught *in*. One Chinese channel in my list captions
every English word the teacher says and leaves the spoken Chinese examples — the part
a learner wants — completely uncaptioned.

So: recover the target-language speech from audio. The constraints that make this
interesting are not the ASR ones.

**Precision over recall, asymmetrically.** Showing a learner an English sentence
labelled as Spanish is a product bug. Missing a Spanish sentence is a shrug — there
are thousands more. I would happily drop half the material to avoid one confident
mistake.

**Short spans are close to worthless.** A learner needs a whole sentence to see how a
word behaves. A 0.4-second fragment where someone says one word and then translates it
is not a usable search result, no matter how correctly it is labelled.

**It runs on a NAS.** The deployment target is a two-core Synology DS923+ with no GPU
that is primarily a file server and has to stay responsive. Whatever I build has to be
measured in tenths of real time on a CPU, not on an A100.

**The method sees audio and a language code. Nothing else.** No titles, no channel
rules, no captions. Anything tuned to the four test clips is a failed experiment.

---

## The obvious approach doesn't work, and fails silently

Whisper has language identification built in. Run it on the file and keep what it says
is Chinese. Two ways to do that, both broken:

| Baseline on one 249 s Chinese lesson | Segments labelled Chinese | Of those, text actually English |
|---|---|---|
| Whole file, `multilingual=True` | 59 of 59 | 35 segments, 158 of 242 s |
| Whole file, `language="zh"` | 72 of 72 | 42 segments, 142 of 232 s |

Whole-file multilingual mode never switched language on this clip. It decided the
video was Chinese and then labelled two and a half minutes of English as Chinese.

Forcing the language is worse, in a way that took me a while to see. Whisper doesn't
refuse out-of-language audio — it **translates it**. Feed it the English line "before
we start, subscribe and like" with `language="zh"` and you get
`我们开始之前,请订阅并按赞`. Fluent, plausible, entirely fabricated as a transcript.
No script check catches it, because the script is correct. No confidence score catches
it, because the model is confident. This is architectural: Whisper conditions on a
single language token per 30-second window, so there is no representation in which
"this window is half English" exists. The behaviour is
[well](https://github.com/openai/whisper/discussions/2285)
[documented](https://github.com/openai/whisper/discussions/49) in the issue tracker and
is not considered a bug.

Scored against human labels later, those two baselines landed at **0.458** and
**0.532** span precision. A coin flip with extra steps.

The conclusion I took from this: **a language decision has to come from the acoustics.**
Text evidence can veto, but it cannot be the primary signal, because the text is
generated by a model that will happily invent the language you asked for.

## What the literature calls this

Before building anything I went looking for the task. It exists, twice, under two names
that measure two different things — and neither measures what I need.

**Language diarization (LD)** is the segmentation task: "which language was spoken
when", with no pre-established boundaries. This is exactly my segmentation problem. It
has real benchmarks:

- The [DISPLACE challenges](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09494) (2023, 2024) cover speaker
  *and* language diarization over 158 hours of far-field multi-speaker Indian
  multilingual conversation. In 2024 the LD baseline scored **29.56 % DER** on the
  evaluation set and the best submission **25.05 %**.
- The [MERLIon CCS challenge](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2023/chua23_interspeech.html)
  (Interspeech 2023) is closer to my audio: English–Mandarin code-switching in
  child-directed speech, non-standard accents, short utterances. The best open-track
  system — an ensemble including Whisper-large — reached **9.5 % EER / 78.9 % balanced
  accuracy**.
- Academic LD systems increasingly fine-tune self-supervised representations; e.g.
  [Frost et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.09645) fine-tune WavLM for five South
  African languages, explicitly to route segments to the right human annotator.

**Language recognition (LRE)** is the detection task, and the framing is exactly mine.
[NIST's evaluations](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=925272)
define it as: given a segment and a *target* language, decide whether the target was
spoken. They score misses against false alarms with an application-weighted cost,
`Cavg`, and [LRE15](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2016/10/06/lre15_evalplan_v23.pdf)
deliberately scored within clusters of closely related languages — the Spanish-vs-Catalan
problem, as a first-class concern, in 2015.

So the task has been formulated. Just never in one piece:

- LD gives me the **granularity** but the wrong **objective**. DER is time-weighted and
  symmetric: a missed second and a wrong second cost the same, and there is no way to
  abstain. A system that drops every short fragment and keeps only long confident runs —
  precisely what I want — scores *badly* under DER while being exactly right for my
  product. DER also has no notion of "this span is 80 % target language", which is the
  single most common outcome in lesson audio.
- LRE gives me the **objective** but the wrong **granularity**. Cavg assumes trials:
  pre-segmented utterances handed to you. Producing the segments is my whole problem.

What I actually need is closer to **selective classification** — the risk-coverage
framing of [El-Yaniv & Wiener](https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/el-yaniv10a.html) and
[Geifman & El-Yaniv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500), where a model may abstain to
guarantee a target error rate — applied to spans rather than to fixed inputs. "Maximise
recall subject to precision ≥ p, and you may refuse to answer" is a coverage-constrained
selective classifier. I have not seen that framing used for language diarization, and I
think it is the right one for anyone doing this for a downstream consumer rather than
for a leaderboard.

There is a third literature that has my *objective* exactly, and hides it in a
methods section: **corpus mining.** VoxLingua107 — the model I ended up using — was
itself [built by mining YouTube](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.12998), and its pipeline
post-filters automatically-labelled segments "to remove segments likely not in the given
language, increasing the proportion of correctly labelled segments to 98 %". That is a
precision-first span extractor with a reported precision number. It is described as data
cleaning, not as a task.

The closest published thing to what I am doing is
[CS-YODAS](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.11514) (2026), which mines 313 hours of in-the-wild
code-switched speech from Creative Commons YouTube across seven matrix languages. Their
pipeline is the mirror image of mine — they use an LLM over *transcripts* where I refuse
to trust transcripts — and they report precision honestly: **18 % from the LLM alone,
70 % after a human-in-the-loop validation round**, ranging from 40 % (Japanese) to 100 %
(Russian). Worth holding on to that 70 % as a reference point for what "mined
code-switched speech at scale" currently costs in precision.

## The pipeline

```
audio → 16 kHz mono → Silero VAD → chunks (≤20 s)
      → VoxLingua107 ECAPA on 2 s windows, hop 0.5 s
      → two-state Viterbi with a switch penalty
      → gate: VoxLingua AND Whisper language ID both ≥ 0.7, span ≥ 1 s
      → Whisper transcript of accepted spans only
```

Three decisions in there carry all the weight.

**Windows, not chunks.** My first version scored whole VAD chunks. It cannot work, and
the reason is mechanical: VAD joins speech across pauses shorter than 0.3 s, and lesson
speech switches language at exactly such pauses. So a chunk is routinely half Chinese
and half English, and no threshold on a whole-chunk score fixes that. On synthetic data
whole-chunk methods never got past 0.76 span precision at any setting. Sliding windows
plus a Viterbi pass that penalises switching gives you boundaries at 0.5 s resolution —
finer than any pause the VAD can see.

**A closed set, not the open one.** VoxLingua107 scores 107 languages. My catalogue has
eleven. Renormalising the probability over just those eleven changed everything for
Spanish — more on why in a moment.

**Two independent votes.** The gate requires both the acoustic model and Whisper's
language ID (over the same closed set) to agree at ≥ 0.7. The acoustic model can't read;
the ASR model can't be trusted alone. Requiring agreement is the cheapest ensemble there
is, and it is the single component that removes the English-glossed spans.

The expensive model only ever sees candidate speech. VAD and VoxLingua run on every
second of audio at well under 0.1× real time on an M1; Whisper runs only on spans that
already passed. That is the shape that makes a NAS plausible — though I have not yet
measured it on the actual NAS, and that number is the one that decides whether any of
this ships.

## Synthetic data first, and why it nearly wasted the experiment

I built a synthetic benchmark before touching real audio: 30 clips, 15 language pairs
including the nasty ones (es↔pt, it↔es, fr↔it), code-switched at known timestamps with
exact ground truth, voice-cloned so the two languages come from the same speaker.

It was useful for exactly two things: proving the plumbing worked, and measuring how
recall depends on utterance length. As a way of *choosing a method*, it was nearly
useless — and I didn't realise that until the real labels arrived.

| Method (best point at span precision ≥ 0.97) | Span precision | Long-run recall |
|---|---|---|
| all five whole-chunk methods | 0.55–0.60 (floor never met) | 0.82–0.89 |
| `refined-vox` | 0.993 | 0.892 |
| `refined-voxpair` | 0.993 | 0.908 |
| `refined-voxset` | 0.994 | 0.985 |
| **`refined-agree` (selected)** | **1.000** | **0.985** |

Four refined methods, all between 0.99 and 1.00 precision, two of them tied to five
decimal places on recall. The ranking was noise. Clean TTS, one speaker per clip, and a
clean pause at every switch make the problem too easy; the choice between the top four
was effectively arbitrary, and I made it with a tie-break rule I had to amend and
disclose.

The lesson I'd generalise: **synthetic benchmarks validate implementations and
characterise failure modes. They do not rank methods that are all near ceiling.** If
your synthetic evaluation cannot separate your candidates, that is not a sign they're
all good — it's a sign the benchmark is spent.

## Labelling 735 units, blind

The real evaluation is four videos: two Chinese lessons and two Spanish lessons, all
taught in English, 41.8 minutes total.

I built a small local labelling server, froze the review units, and labelled every one
of them myself: audio only, one unit at a time, five keys — *target / other / mixed /
no speech / unsure*. No transcript visible, no detector score, no indication of what the
method had decided. 735 units, all judged: **272 target, 331 other, 129 mixed, 2 no
speech, 1 unsure.**

One thing I got right by accident and would now do deliberately: **the labelling units
are not the method's units.** Review units are raw VAD pieces, never merged across a
pause, capped at 6 s. Method spans are cut at Viterbi window midpoints. Keeping them
independent means the labels are reusable ground truth for any future method, and the
labelling stays blind. It also creates the central measurement problem of the whole
experiment, which is the next section.

The honest caveats: one annotator, who is also the person who built the system, so there
is no inter-annotator agreement and no way to rule out my own drift. Only 3 units of 735
were genuinely hard to call. The ambiguity is not in the reviewer — it is in `mixed`.

## Results

The operating point chosen on synthetic data, applied unchanged:

| Clip | Target | Spans | Judged | Span precision | Hard failures | Time precision | Long-run recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chinese A | zh | 33 | 30 | **1.000** | 0 | 1.000 | 0.777 |
| Chinese B | zh | 26 | 21 | 0.952 | 0 | 0.982 | 0.852 |
| Spanish A | es | 27 | 25 | **1.000** | 0 | 0.996 | 0.800 |
| Spanish B | es | 66 | 52 | 0.923 | 0 | 0.966 | 0.777 |
| **Pooled** | | 152 | 128 | **0.961** (123/128) | **0** | 0.986 | 0.792 |

"Judged" is smaller than "spans" because a span lying entirely over `mixed` units can't
be scored either way. A span counts as correct if ≥ 80 % of the judged speech under it
is target language, and as a hard failure at ≤ 50 %.

**0.961, and zero hard failures.** Not one accepted span is majority English. Against
0.458 and 0.532 for the Whisper baselines on the same audio.

It also **misses the 0.97 precision floor I pre-registered** on synthetic data, where it
scored 1.000. I'm leaving that as a miss. Raising the threshold from 0.7 to 0.8 would
give 0.975 — but these are the only real labels in existence, and spending them on
tuning would leave me with no unbiased estimate of anything. The honest version is: the
floor was set on easy data, real data is harder, and the fix has to be validated on
clips labelled afterwards.

All five imprecise spans are the same failure mode, and small:

| Span | Target / other | Transcript |
|---|---|---|
| 2.3 s | 1.7 / 0.5 s | `我是新来的` |
| 3.2 s | 2.1 / 0.9 s | `NONE. No quedaba nada.` |
| 1.8 s | 0.9 / 0.7 s | `Menos less.` |
| 3.0 s | 1.6 / 1.0 s | `¿Por qué puedo? Porque hay gente. ¿Cuándo?` |
| 3.2 s | 2.3 / 0.7 s | `Only. Solo me gusta el chocolate.` |

A correct example sentence with an English gloss stuck to it. Which, for a learner, is
a mildly annoying result rather than a wrong one — but it's the thing to fix next.

## The measurement problem: what a label can't say

129 of 735 units are `mixed` — both languages audible inside one unit, usually "the word
认识 means to know". A mixed unit records *that* both languages are present. It cannot
record *where* the switch is, because it's a raw VAD piece with no internal structure.

The method cuts on a finer grid than the labels do. So when an accepted span overlaps a
mixed unit, the label genuinely cannot tell me whether the method took the right half.

You can score this two ways, and both are defensible:

| Reading | Judged spans | Span precision | Hard failures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lenient**: mixed time is unjudgeable, excluded | 128 | **0.961** | 0 |
| **Strict**: every mixed second is other-language | 152 | **0.763** | 26 |

That is an enormous range, and I think reporting a single number here would be
dishonest. But the evidence isn't symmetric between them. Across the 130 mixed units
(523 s), accepted spans leave **91 untouched, cut into 31, and swallow 8 whole** —
taking **17.4 % of mixed time**. The method is clearly *trying* to cut inside mixed
units, which is what the strict reading refuses to give it credit for.

For Chinese there's a tiebreaker that needs neither the labels nor the detectors: count
Han characters in the transcript. **55 of 59 Chinese spans are ≥ 90 % Han**, mean
target-script share 0.95–0.99. The strict reading badly overstates contamination there.

For Spanish, where the two languages share a script, no such check exists, and the gap
stays open. That's the concrete argument for the next step: a CTC verifier. A
Spanish-conditioned CTC model given English audio emits low-confidence phonetic garbage
rather than fluent Spanish — a failure you can *detect*, unlike Whisper's.

## Span length is the whole story

This is the result I'd keep if I could keep only one.

| Accepted span length | Spans | Span precision |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 s | 26 | 0.960 |
| 2–3 s | 51 | 0.975 |
| 3–5 s | 55 | 0.933 |
| 5–10 s | 16 | **1.000** |
| ≥ 10 s | 4 | **1.000** |

| Target-run length | Runs | Time recall |
|---|---|---|
| < 1 s | 15 | 0.069 |
| 1–2 s | 28 | 0.397 |
| 2–3 s | 38 | 0.612 |
| 3–5 s | 50 | 0.749 |
| 5–10 s | 15 | **0.819** |
| ≥ 10 s | 8 | **0.820** |

**Of the 20 accepted spans of 5 s or more, all 18 that could be judged are correct.**
Recall on runs of 5 s or more is 0.82. Recall on runs under a second is 0.07 — and
those 15 sub-second runs total *twelve seconds* across 42 minutes of audio.

Both curves point the same way, and that is the argument for reporting them. A single
pooled precision number is an average over spans whose usefulness differs by an order of
magnitude. Under DER, dropping those sub-second runs is pure error. Under my product,
it's free. If you are building something like this, **stratify by duration before you
tune anything** — the aggregate number will otherwise push you to optimise the part of
the distribution you don't care about.

## Where the detector was wrong, and why it wasn't what I thought

On the Spanish clips I kept seeing VoxLingua score plain Spanish as something else. A
clean `cuando era estudiante` came back **Latin 0.93, Spanish 0.04**, while Whisper's
language ID said Spanish 0.99. Catalan and Esperanto turned up too. The open-set score
alone accepted 4 spans on one clip where the closed-set score accepted 28.

My first hypothesis was that there was actual Catalan in the audio. Then I labelled all
four videos end to end and can state flatly that there isn't: across 42 minutes there are
exactly two languages per clip, English plus the target, and no third language anywhere.

So these are **detector confusions on real speech, not content**. And there is a paper
that predicts this precisely. Bafna & Wiesner (2025),
[*LID Models are Actually Accent Classifiers*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00628), argue
that LID systems key on accent rather than on language, and report that "Catalan-accented
English is often identified as Catalan". Their numbers for the same architecture I'm
using: ECAPA-TDNN at **87.6 % on mainstream accents, 55.8 % on L2 accents**.

My clips are a natural experiment in that claim. One Spanish teacher is a native
Peninsular speaker; the other is an American with a strong, immediately audible accent
in Spanish. The Chinese teachers are native. The lowest span precision of the four clips
(0.923) and four of the five imprecise spans belong to the L2 Spanish teacher.

Two things follow. First, the closed set isn't a hack — it's a targeted fix for a known
model pathology, and it recovers most of the genuine Spanish that the open-set score
throws away. Second, and less comfortably: **a detector that already places native and
L2 Spanish near Catalan and Latin cannot be assumed to reject actual Catalan.** My closed
set removes Catalan from the competitors by construction, which means I have no evidence
about the case that worries me most. Testing that with an impostor set of real Catalan,
Galician and Portuguese speech is the top of the next round.

## What I'd tell someone building this

1. **Don't let a generative model make the language decision.** Whisper will translate
   out-of-language audio into fluent target text with high confidence. Use it as a
   second vote on a span, never as the detector.
2. **Decide at a finer granularity than your VAD.** Language switches happen at pauses
   too short for VAD to split on. Whole-segment classification tops out around 0.76
   precision on this material no matter how you threshold it.
3. **Renormalise over the languages you actually have.** Open-set LID over 107 languages
   spends its probability mass on neighbours of your target, especially for accented
   speech.
4. **Make your labels method-independent.** Label VAD units, not your system's output.
   The labels outlive the method, and the labelling stays blind. Accept in exchange that
   you will have an ambiguity class you can't resolve, and report it as a range.
5. **Stratify every metric by duration.** If short and long spans have different value
   to you, a pooled number is actively misleading.
6. **Say which number is the honest one.** I have a result that reads 0.961 and a result
   that reads 0.763 on the same spans, and the difference is entirely a scoring
   convention.

## What I have not shown

- **No impostor test.** The Catalan/Galician/Portuguese case — the one the accent-LID
  literature says is the real risk — is untested, and my closed set removes it by
  construction.
- **No NAS measurement.** Every cost number is from an M1 laptop, and some of it is
  inflated by redundant decoding during the sweep. The two-core target is unmeasured,
  and it is a promotion blocker.
- **Four clips, two languages, one annotator.** No street speech, no music, no
  overlapping speakers, no second opinion on any label.
- **The clips are a stress case, not a sample.** They're lessons *about* a language,
  taught in English, alternating every few seconds — roughly 5 % of their speech is
  target-language example material. I'd exclude channels like these from the corpus.
  That makes them a hard test of switch detection and a poor predictor of recall on the
  mostly-target-language channels I actually want to index, where runs are long and
  switches are rare.
- **The 0.97 floor is unmet** at the operating point in use, and the obvious fix is one
  I've deliberately not applied.
